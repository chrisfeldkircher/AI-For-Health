\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Detecting whether a speaker has a cold from short audio clips is the kind of paralinguistic task where modern foundation-model representations should help and where the standard practice for fine-tuning them creates two persistent problems. The first is the speaker shortcut: in small-data corpora with a few hundred speakers and a binary label that correlates partially with voice identity, a classifier trained end-to-end on a foundation-model representation can score well on a per-utterance speaker recognition probe and only modestly on the actual paralinguistic task~\cite{huckvale2017cold,schuller2017interspeech}. The second is the lack of a clean methodology for measuring whether a proposed de-confounding intervention has done anything: speaker probes vary across substrates, dimensionality, and probe architecture, and an apparent ``probe top-1 dropped from $X$ to $Y$'' result can be an artefact of the bottleneck the intervention introduced rather than de-confounding signal.

Rather than treating URTIC as a fresh novelty benchmark, this paper uses the ComParE 2017 Cold sub-challenge~\cite{schuller2017interspeech} as a structured revisit. We begin from ideas that were already reasonable in the 2017 participant ecosystem, use them to build challenge-grounded baselines, and then ask three questions: do modern frozen speech representations add useful signal, does constrained late fusion add complementary information, and which apparent gains survive stronger speaker-aware and robustness-oriented controls?

Within a frozen-backbone setting and a modest compute budget ($\sim$8.5\,k chunks, $k{=}210$ pseudo-speakers), the positive system combines a frozen WavLM-Large~\cite{chen2022wavlm} representation with a layer-weighted classification head on cached pooled statistics, a per-layer cold-vs-speaker audit, and a late-fusion stage that admits low-dimensional handcrafted feature groups only when they improve the cold task without obvious honesty regressions. Around that system, we test three de-confounding families that are intuitively plausible on this task: cross-speaker augmentation, supervised contrastive learning with speaker-masked positives, and gradient-reversal speaker adversaries. None improves over the simpler cold-CE bottleneck within the tested scope, which makes the negative results part of the main story rather than a side note.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We argue three contributions, in increasing order of transferability.

\textbf{C1: A controlled, audited positive system from frozen speech representations.} On a speaker-grouped protocol stricter than the original challenge, a frozen WavLM-Large head with an \emph{honesty-prior} layer-weight initialisation --- each backbone layer weighted by its cold gain minus its speaker gain, the single most reusable modelling component here --- plus a constrained, honesty-gated K=2 late fusion reaches per-seed devel\_test UAR $0.7037 \pm 0.0060$ at $N{=}5$. Reported shadow-first, the multi-K ensemble attains $0.6940 \pm 0.0157$ across 10 alternative speaker-disjoint devel partitions; under the \emph{same} protocol a classical ComParE-2016 $+$ LR control attains $0.5853 \pm 0.0083$, an unambiguous $\Delta\,{+}0.109$ same-protocol margin. On the single fixed canonical partition the system reaches $0.7111$, adjacent to the 2017 hidden-test $0.710$ --- one favourable partition, not an expected-test claim. Multi-K is promoted only because it cleared the same pre-registered shadow-admission gate that a classical K=3 fusion candidate failed.

\textbf{C2: A trustworthy-evaluation protocol for small, speaker-confounded paralinguistic corpora.} Because the 4students release omits speaker IDs, we reconstruct them as ECAPA-TDNN pseudo-speakers and \emph{validate the shipped labels in their own raw embedding space} (kNN label cohesion $94\times$ above chance; an independent clustering recovers a consistent partition), then enforce speaker-grouped splits, substrate-matched speaker probes, and a cheap shadow-split replay that quantifies single-partition devel-side overfit risk without hidden-test access. The shadow distribution, not the canonical point, is our binding metric --- a transferable recipe for any small-data corpus where the test set is withheld.

\textbf{C3: An audit-driven account of what survives stronger controls, and what does not.} This is the paper's strongest contribution. Cross-speaker mixup, speaker-masked contrastive de-confounding, and gradient-reversal speaker adversaries all fail to beat the simpler cold-CE pipeline once speaker-grouped splits, negative controls, and shadow-split robustness are enforced. Those failures are the payload: they produce the M8--M19 discipline set, including self-splice controls, bottleneck-confound checks, discriminator-ceiling diagnostics, and shadow-split robustness analysis. The paper's main methodological claim is therefore not ``benchmark closure,'' but a more reliable map of which conclusions on URTIC remain credible after stronger auditing.
